1. A model of skew

Skew limits speedup, whatever the cluster

From Lecture 5: \(T \geq t_{\max}\), and a key with a fraction \(f\) of the rows forces a partition with at least \(f \cdot n\) rows, for any \(R\).

If a task’s time is proportional to its rows, then \(t_{\max} \geq f \cdot W\), and the speedup is bounded by:

\[S = \frac{W}{T} \;\leq\; \frac{W}{f \cdot W} = \frac{1}{f}\]

\(f\) (hottest key) Maximum speedup of that stage
40% 2.5×, with 10 or 10,000 cores
10% 10×
1% 100×

It has the same form as Amdahl’s law: the hot key behaves like the serial fraction of the stage. Adding cores cannot help, and that is what makes skew different from a simple shortage of resources.

The model against real data

Lab 5: \(n\) = 5,000,000 rows, 40% with customer_id = 0, the rest uniform, \(R = 16\). The hot partition holds the hot key plus its share of the rest:

\[\text{max} = f n + \frac{(1-f)\,n}{R} = 2{,}000{,}000 + 187{,}500 = 2{,}187{,}500 \qquad \text{median} = \frac{(1-f)\,n}{R} = 187{,}500\]

Model Measured in Lab 5
Largest partition 2,187,500 2,186,568
Median partition 187,500 187,215
Ratio 11.7× 11.7×

The model predicts the ratio from just \(f\) and \(R\):

\[\frac{\text{max}}{\text{median}} = 1 + \frac{f\,R}{1-f}\]

So the ratio grows with \(R\). Raising shuffle.partitions makes the imbalance look worse and does not shrink the largest partition: it still holds at least \(f n\) rows.

Where hot keys come from

Natural distributions. Popularity usually follows something like a Zipf law, where the \(k\)-th most frequent key has:

\[f_k = \frac{1/k}{H_d}, \qquad H_d = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{1}{i} \approx \ln d + 0.577\]

With \(d = 10^6\) keys: \(H_d \approx 14.4\), so the top key has 6.9% of the rows (speedup at most 14×), and the top 100 keys hold 36%. A million distinct keys do not guarantee balance.

Artificial values, often the worst and the easiest to fix:

Value Typical origin Action
NULL key Optional field Filter it or handle it apart
0, -1, "unknown" Placeholder defaults Find it and treat it as missing
A giant account Marketplace, bot, test user Separate it

An inner join already discards NULL keys (the plan shows isnotnull), yet a LEFT JOIN must keep them on the left. All of those rows hash to the same partition, with no match to gain from it.

Does skew affect every operation?

No, and the reason is the previous lecture’s partial aggregation.

Operation With a hot key Why
groupBy().sum() Almost immune The partial step leaves the key at most \(M\) rows
join Suffers All its rows meet in one task; output \(\sum_k a_k b_k\)
collect_list, percentile Suffers Not decomposable: all values travel
Window with partitionBy(k) Suffers The whole key must be in one partition, sorted

A hot key in a groupBy is not necessarily a problem: first look at which operator is stalled.

The worst case is a join with hot keys on both sides: \(a_k = 10^6\) and \(b_k = 10^3\) give \(10^9\) rows in a single task.

2. Fixes and their costs

Salting: why it is correct, and what it costs

Add to each row of \(R\) a random \(s \in \{0,\dots,k-1\}\), replicate each row of \(S\) \(k\) times with \(s = 0,\dots,k-1\), and join on \((\text{key}, s)\).

Correctness. Each row of \(R\) has one \(s\), and each matching row of \(S\) has exactly one copy with that \(s\). So every pair appears once, and the output is unchanged. In Lab 5: 5,000,000 rows with or without salt.

Effect. The hot key’s rows spread over \(k\) subkeys. To bring each chunk below the average load \(n/R\):

\[\frac{f n}{k} \leq \frac{n}{R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k \geq f R\]

Cost. \(S\) grows from \(|S|\) to \(k|S|\) in the shuffle. With \(f = 40\%\), \(R = 200\): \(k \geq 80\), or 80 copies of all of \(S\).

Selective salting: salt only the known hot keys, with \(s = 0\) for the rest. The extra cost becomes \((k - 1) |S_{\text{hot}}|\). With one hot customer, that is 79 extra rows instead of 79 extra copies of a table.

Exercise: why 1.4× and not 1.0×?

Lab 5 with \(k = 16\), \(R = 16\) measured: largest 441,645, median 308,298, ratio 1.4×. Perfect balance would be 312,500 everywhere. Can you explain those numbers?

Each of the 16 salt values carries \(2{,}000{,}000 / 16 =\) 125,000 hot rows, and it lands on a partition by hash: 16 balls into 16 bins, not one each. A partition that receives \(j\) salts holds:

\[j \times 125{,}000 + 187{,}500\]

\(j\) Model Measured
1 312,500 308,298 (median)
2 437,500 441,645 (largest)

So two salts collided in one partition. Salt spreads the key among \(k\) subkeys, but the partitioner places them with collisions: from Lecture 5, about \(e^{-1} \approx 37\%\) of the bins stay without any salt.

To improve it: \(k \gg R\) makes collisions matter less, but it also multiplies \(S\) by more copies. There is no free option here.

Choosing a fix

Fix Added cost Fails when
Clean the key (NULL, placeholders) None: reduces \(n\) The key is genuinely frequent
Broadcast \(N \cdot |S|\) of memory \(S\) is large, or the preserved side is the small one
Separate the hot key + union Code, and a list of keys to maintain Hot keys change or are many
Selective salting \((k-1)|S_{\text{hot}}|\) Same: needs to know the keys
Full salting \((k-1)|S|\) \(S\) is large; \(k\) becomes costly
AQE skew join Replicates part of \(S\) Not a join, unsupported side, or few map tasks

Apply them in that order. First ask if the rows are needed at all, since removing them lowers \(W\) and \(f\) at once.

Do not use salting in a groupBy by reflex: decomposable aggregations are already protected by the partial step, and a non-decomposable one (median, countDistinct) gives wrong results if you aggregate the salted groups and combine them.

3. Cache: when it pays off

An inequality for caching

A DataFrame is used by \(k\) actions. Let \(C\) = cost to compute it, \(M\) = extra cost to store it, \(L\) = cost to read it back.

\[\underbrace{k\,C}_{\text{without cache}} \quad\text{vs}\quad \underbrace{C + M + (k-1)\,L}_{\text{with cache}}\]

Cache pays off if and only if:

\[(k - 1)\,(C - L) > M\]

Consequence Why
\(k = 1\) never pays off The left side is zero
\(C \leq L\) never pays off A plain Parquet read: reading again is about as cheap
A larger \(C\) justifies a smaller \(k\) Multi-way joins, heavy aggregations

Lab 5 ran both cases, with these totals:

Case Without With
Small result of an expensive aggregation 2.44 s 0.78 + 0.20 = 0.98 s
Wide table, cheap to recompute 1.45 s 1.84 s

The hidden cost: memory and pruning

Caching a wide DataFrame loses column pruning. Without cache, each action reads only its columns from Parquet. With cache, everything is computed and stored first. So \(L\) is cheap, but \(M\) pays for every column. Select the columns you need before cache().

Cache competes with execution in the same memory region (Spark tuning guide):

\[M_{\text{unified}} = (\text{heap} - 300\ \text{MiB}) \times 0.6 \qquad R_{\text{protected}} = M_{\text{unified}} \times 0.5\]

With an 8 GiB heap: \(M_{\text{unified}} \approx\) 4.6 GiB, of which about 2.3 GiB are protected from eviction.

Rule Consequence
Execution may evict storage, down to \(R\) A big shuffle can remove cached blocks
Storage may not evict execution Cache only uses what execution leaves free
Above \(R\), cached blocks are in danger Then LRU applies, and the \(C\) you saved is paid again

So the cache can slow the job down: within \(R\), those 2.3 GiB are no longer available to the sorts and hash tables, which then spill to disk.

Cache, checkpoint or write?

cache() / persist() checkpoint() Write Parquet, read back
Where Executors’ memory/disk Reliable storage Reliable storage
Lineage Kept Cut Cut
A lost executor Recompute from lineage Reread Reread
Survives the application No No, unless reused explicitly Yes
Typical use Several actions in one job Very long lineage (iterative) Intermediate step of a pipeline

Cache is not a fault-tolerance mechanism. A lost cached partition is simply recomputed, and with a long lineage containing wide dependencies, that recomputation can be very expensive. The RDD paper recommends checkpointing exactly in that case (Zaharia et al., 2012, §5.4).

Storage levels. df.cache() uses MEMORY_AND_DISK, whereas rdd.cache() uses MEMORY_ONLY: the same word, with different defaults. cache() is also lazy: it only stores data at the first action, and unpersist() releases it.

4. Adaptive Query Execution

The idea: stages are measurement points

Lecture 5 showed that the static plan has very little information: only file sizes, and a Filter is assumed to remove nothing.

But a shuffle materializes its output on disk, and at that moment Spark knows the exact size of each partition. AQE re-plans the rest of the query with those numbers:

 plan (estimates) ─▶ run stages with no pending parents ─▶ shuffle output on disk
       ▲                                                         │  real sizes
       └────────────── re-optimize what has not run ◀────────────┘
Feature Before After
Coalesce partitions 200 partitions, mostly tiny Few, by merging contiguous ones
Switch join Sort-merge (size overestimated) Broadcast hash join
Skew join One giant task Several tasks, replicating the other side

Why “contiguous”: merging partitions \(i\) and \(i+1\) of a hash partitioning is always correct, because rows with the same key remain together. It only changes how many tasks read them.

Its limit is structural: AQE only knows what has already been shuffled. It cannot change the first stage, and it cannot help a query with no Exchange.

Coalescing: what target does it really use?

spark.sql.adaptive.advisoryPartitionSizeInBytes (64 MB) looks like the target, yet by default it is not respected when coalescing:

Setting Default Effect
coalescePartitions.parallelismFirst true Ignores the 64 MB and computes a target from the cluster’s parallelism, usually smaller
coalescePartitions.minPartitionSize 1 MB The lower limit that then applies

Why: the aim is not to lose parallelism compared with running without AQE. The documentation recommends false on a busy cluster, so as not to produce many small tasks.

Check in Lab 5: groupBy("region") has 5 rows after partial aggregation. Without AQE, 200 partitions; with AQE, 1. It is below every limit, so everything is merged.

Reading the plan: before running, explain() shows isFinalPlan=false, the initial estimate. The real decisions appear in the plan as AQEShuffleRead only once the query has run.

Switching the join: useful, but not free

If at runtime one side is below spark.sql.adaptive.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold (default: same as the static one, 10 MB), AQE turns the sort-merge into a broadcast.

Example from Lecture 5: a 1 GB table filtered down to 1 MB. The static plan does not see the filter’s effect, so it chooses sort-merge. Once the small side’s shuffle has run, its real size appears.

Sort-merge Broadcast from AQE Broadcast from the start
Shuffle map of large side Done Already done Avoided
Network for large side Yes No: read locally No
Sort of both sides Yes Avoided Avoided

So the documentation says it is “not as efficient as planning a broadcast hash join in the first place”. The map stage of the large side has already been paid.

Practical consequence: if you know a side is small after filtering, F.broadcast() is still better than waiting for AQE to discover it.

Skew join: the exact rule

A partition is skewed if its size exceeds both limits (OptimizeSkewedJoin):

\[\text{size} > \max\big(\texttt{skewedPartitionThresholdInBytes},\ \ \texttt{skewedPartitionFactor} \times \text{median}\big)\]

with defaults of 256 MB and 5.0. It is split into pieces of about:

\[\text{target} = \max\big(\texttt{advisoryPartitionSizeInBytes},\ \text{average of non-skewed partitions}\big)\]

Exercise. Median 100 MB, average of the others 100 MB, default 64 MB advisory. Which are split, and into how many?

Threshold: \(\max(256, 500) = 500\) MB.

Partition Skewed? Pieces
400 MB No: 4× the median, but below the threshold 1
2 GB Yes \(2000 / 100 =\) 20 tasks, with the other side’s partition replicated 20 times

The rule is deliberately conservative: below 256 MB, a straggler does not justify replicating.

Skew join: how it splits, and when it cannot

It splits the partition by map output ranges, and the other side is replicated once per range:

 R, partition 7 (hot): Map0 Map1 │ Map2 Map3 │ Map4       S, partition 7
                       └──task A─┘ └──task B─┘ └task C┘   ──▶ copied to A, B and C

Reading different map outputs lets one key be split with no salt at all. It also explains its limits:

Limit Why
At most \(M\) pieces The granularity is a map output: a key from a single map task cannot be split
Skew on both sides Cross product of splits: \(p \times q\) tasks
LEFT splits only the left The preserved side cannot be replicated: same reason as the broadcast restriction
RIGHT splits only the right Same, mirrored
Only joins In a groupBy, all of a key’s rows must end up together, or the result would be wrong
May be skipped If it would add a shuffle, unless forceOptimizeSkewedJoin is on

5. UDFs

What a UDF costs

1. Crossing the boundary. Native functions run inside the generated code of the JVM. A Python UDF sends data to a separate Python worker and back:

 F.when(...)    JVM ───────────────────────────────▶ JVM
 @udf           JVM ─▶ pickle ─▶ Python, per value ─▶ pickle ─▶ JVM
 @pandas_udf    JVM ─▶ Arrow batches ─▶ Pandas, per batch ─▶ Arrow ─▶ JVM

With \(n\) rows, batches of \(B\) (default 10,000), per-row call cost \(g\) and per-batch transfer cost \(b\):

\[T_{\text{row}} \approx n\,(s + g) \qquad T_{\text{pandas}} \approx \frac{n}{B}\,(b + g_{\text{vec}})\]

2. Opacity, the larger cost. Catalyst cannot see inside a UDF:

  • No predicate pushdown through it, and no constant folding.
  • A filter on its result runs after computing it for every row.
  • It is assumed deterministic: it may run more or fewer times than written. Use asNondeterministic() otherwise.
  • No short-circuit guarantee: a guard like F.when(x.isNotNull(), my_udf(x)) does not ensure nulls are not passed to it.

Exercise: why was the Pandas UDF the slowest?

The usual ranking is native > Pandas UDF > Python UDF. Lab 5 measured, on 5 million rows and a simple function returning text:

Method Time
Native F.when 0.36 s
Python UDF 0.65 s
Pandas UDF 1.67 s

The model says it depends on \(g\) versus \(g_{\text{vec}}\). Here the function is three comparisons, so \(g\) is tiny, and there is little per-row work for vectorization to save. Meanwhile the Pandas version pays for Arrow conversion, and for building a Series of Python strings per batch.

The likely cause is this cost structure; the Lab does not isolate it. Vectorization pays off when the function does real numeric work per row, and it pays less with text output.

What holds without exception: native is the fastest, and it is the only option the optimizer understands. Between the two UDFs, measure with your function, rather than rely on a ranking. Spark also offers Arrow-optimized Python UDFs (useArrow=True), which run row by row but transfer batches.

6. Diagnosis from the Spark UI

From symptom to model term

Metric (Stages tab) What it measures If it is high
Duration, max vs median \(t_{\max}\) vs typical Skew, or a slow node
Shuffle Read Size, max vs median Distribution of \(D\) Data skew, if it tracks the duration
Shuffle Write Size Volume \(D\) Too much data moved: filter, aggregate or broadcast sooner
Spill (Memory / Disk) Execution memory exceeded Too few partitions, or a very large one
GC Time JVM pressure Too many objects, or too much cached
Scheduler Delay Overhead \(o\) Too many tiny tasks
Locality Level Where each task ran Reads with ANY suggest locality was lost

Max and median duration differ, with even data sizes? The cause is not data skew: look for a slow node, GC or heavy per-row work on certain values. Speculative execution helps only in the first case (spark.speculation, default false).

Integrating problem: diagnose this stage

A sort-merge join stage, 200 tasks, 100 cores, AQE skew join off:

Metric Median Max
Duration 20 s 600 s
Shuffle Read 100 MB 3 GB
  1. What are \(W\) and the bounds on \(T\)? 2. What is the most that more cores could gain? 3. What would be gained by eliminating the skew? 4. With AQE on, is it split, and into how many?
  1. \(W \approx 199 \times 20 + 600 = 4{,}580\) s, \(W/c = 45.8\) s. Bounds: \(600 \leq T \leq 45.8 + 0.99 \times 600 \approx 640\) s.
  2. Nothing: \(T \geq t_{\max} = 600\) s with any number of cores.
  3. With \(t_{\max}\) around the median, \(T \approx\) 46 s to 66 s: about 10×, with the same hardware.
  4. \(3\ \text{GB} > \max(256\ \text{MB}, 500\ \text{MB})\): skewed. Target \(\max(64, 100) = 100\) MB, so about 30 tasks, provided there are at least that many map outputs carrying the key. That would be \(t \approx 20\) s per piece.

If it were collect_list in place of a join, AQE could not split it, so the fix would have to be in the query.
